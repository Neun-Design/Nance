# Where Nance comes from

> **"You cannot delegate, scale or audit work that is not written down."**

That sentence is not a slogan. It is the conclusion of a programme that nearly failed for
exactly this reason.

## Smart Outsourcing

Ten years ago, Neun Design ran a programme called Smart Outsourcing. The model was simple and
sound: prepare and train engineering professionals to be hired by clients, sized precisely to
the workload being outsourced. The client got capacity that was already trained on their
domain; the professionals got a career path.

## The fragility that almost broke it

The programme kept running into the same wall, and it was never where anyone expected.

Clients had **no clear processes or procedures for most of the tasks they wanted to
outsource**. Not because they were badly run — many were excellent operations — but because
the knowledge of how the work was actually done lived in the people doing it.

That single gap made three things nearly impossible at once:

- **Finding qualified people** — you cannot specify a profile for work you cannot describe
- **Onboarding them** — there was nothing to onboard *onto*, only colleagues to shadow
- **Training them** — every new person restarted the same informal transfer, at full cost

The bottleneck was never talent supply. It was that the work itself was undocumented.

## The origin of Nance

To protect the programme, Neun began building the system that would make processes,
procedures and competences **explicit** — registered, owned, linked to the work they govern,
and usable by someone who did not grow up inside the operation.

That system is the MVP of nance.it.

## Why the origin matters

It explains a design choice that surprises people who arrive expecting a quality-management
tool: **knowledge is the foundation, not a module**.

Most QMS products treat documented information as an output — something you produce so that
an audit goes well. Here it is the input. Nothing else in the model works without it: you
cannot certify a competence against a procedure that does not exist, cannot staff a job
against a competence nobody holds, cannot generate evidence for work nobody defined.

Nance was built by an organisation whose business depended on the answer to one question —
*can someone who did not write this be trusted to execute it?* — and the platform is what
that question produced.
